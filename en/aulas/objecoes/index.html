Lesson 6 · 19 min

Objections: the receipt for an earlier failure

There is a whole industry teaching you to "break objections" with a catchy line. The view here is different: a price objection is the receipt for a failure that happened earlier. Either the problem was never measured, or trust was missing. If the earlier work was done well, the final conversation is about logistics, and almost never about convincing.

Start the lesson

In this lesson you will

  1. 1Tell apart the only two causes of a price objection
  2. 2Do the math on the problem in front of the client
  3. 3State the price and follow it with the next step
  4. 4Practice in a simulated conversation with a scoreboard

Goal: read an objection as an earlier failure, measure the problem before the price, and respond by reducing the scope, not by discounting.

    03 · The four rules after the number

    They apply with or without an objection.

    1. Give the price and move on

    Silence after the number turns the price into an event and forces the other person to react.

    Say the amount in the same tone as the rest of the conversation and, in the same breath, add the payment terms and what happens next.

    How it sounds"For this scope, it comes to this amount. Most people pay in two installments. If it makes sense, I'll send you the summary today and we start on Monday. Shall I reserve the week?"

    2. Scope goes down, price doesn't

    A discount becomes the new starting point: at renewal, the client remembers the discounted price.

    Cut scope: one channel instead of three, one branch instead of the whole company, a two-week pilot instead of three months.

    3. The next step, inside the meeting

    "When are you available?" hands the work over, and the easiest way out wins: postponing.

    "Wednesday at 10 a.m. or Thursday at 3 p.m.?" asks for a choice. Send the invite while you're talking and ask them to confirm it arrived.

    4. Value decays over time

    A proposal that arrives days later gets read without the conversation that justified it: the price stays, the value evaporates.

    Send it the same day, open with the measured problem, keep it to one or two pages, and book the review at the same time.

    04 · Templates to copy

    You run the prompt right after the conversation. The answers stay open next to it in the next lesson.

    Prompt · problem-size calculator
    You are my sales support. Below are the notes from a conversation
with a potential client. Do the following, in this order:
1. Calculate the size of the problem per month, with the calculation step by step
   (frequency × unit value × working days). Mark every estimate
   as "assumed" and list what I still need to ask.
2. Tell me whether my price is a small, visible fraction of that number. If it
   isn't, tell me frankly that this proposal should not be made.
3. Write the price sentence in at most 3 sentences: value + payment
   terms + next step with two date options.
4. Write the smaller-scope version, same price per unit of delivery.

NOTES
sector: <e.g., home renovation company>
reported problem: <e.g., calls from new clients going unanswered>
frequency: <e.g., 5 per day>
value of one closed client: <amount>
what I offer: <diagnosis / review / advisory / teaching>
my price: <amount>
    Script · the four most common objections
    1) "IT'S TOO EXPENSIVE."
"I understand, it really is not a small amount. My suggestion: we start
with just <the most painful part>, which costs a lot less. You see how I
work and then we revisit the rest. Does that make sense?"
→ reduces SCOPE. Never: "I can do it for half".

2) "I'LL THINK ABOUT IT."
"Of course. Just so I can help you think it through: what was less clear,
the result, the timeline or the price? I'll send you a one-page summary
today and we talk for 15 minutes on Thursday or Friday. Which do you prefer?"
→ turns the postponement into a date.

3) "WE ALREADY HAVE SOMEONE WHO DOES THAT."
"Great, that means the problem is real. What still bothers you
even with <the current solution>? If everything is running smoothly, I'll be the
first to tell you not to change anything."
→ honest verdict.

4) "NOW IS NOT A PRIORITY."
"I understand. When does this usually become a priority? After <event>?
I'll reach out in <agreed timeframe> and put it on the calendar right now."
→ leave with the next date, even if it's 90 days away.
